Project Summary Overview

This project is an electrical resistivity tomography (ERT) instrument built for the Technology Student Association (TSA) Engineering Design national competition. It produces subsurface resistivity maps — pseudo-section images — from an array of surface electrodes driven into soil, enabling detection of buried voids, metallic objects, and density anomalies without excavation.

The instrument targets the NAE Grand Challenge "Engineer the Tools of Scientific Discovery." It was designed from first principles to be manufacturable for under $250 — roughly 50–100× cheaper than commercial ERT systems — making it viable for post-disaster site assessment, archaeological survey, and geohazard screening in resource-limited regions.

Core achievement

A four-electrode Wenner array ERT system with a 16-bit ADC measurement chain, a single-supply instrumentation amplifier (INA333, G=1001), a Howland constant-current pump with a MT3608 12V compliance rail, full I²C-controlled electrode role-switching via MCP23017 + CD74HC4067 muxes, and a custom HTML pseudo-section visualization tool — all controlled by a Teensy 4.0 microcontroller and producing valid apparent resistivity data (ρ = 2πa·V/I) from real soil.

Motivation & Grand Challenge Context

The problem

Subsurface hazard assessment — checking whether ground is safe to build on after an earthquake, flood, or landslide — currently requires either expensive geophysical instruments (commercial ERT systems: $5,000–$50,000) or physical excavation. In post-disaster housing contexts, particularly across South and Southeast Asia, neither option is reliably available. Families rebuild on unsafe ground because they have no affordable way to verify it.

The same instrument gap affects paleontology, archaeology, and infrastructure inspection in developing regions. Existing open-source ERT projects require PCB fabrication and significant electronics expertise.

The opportunity

ERT is a mature, well-understood technique whose cost is primarily in electronics — not physics. A careful selection of commodity microcontrollers, ADCs, and amplifiers can produce a field-capable instrument at a fraction of the commercial price, with the measurement mathematics identical to any professional system.

NAE Grand Challenge alignment

This project addresses "Engineer the Tools of Scientific Discovery" by creating a low-cost, open-source instrument that makes a previously inaccessible measurement technique available to researchers, first responders, and communities worldwide. The design philosophy — commodity parts, documented firmware, open schematics — is intended to enable local replication.

Scope

The instrument is scoped to void/density-anomaly detection: differentiating resistive voids (air gaps, plastic, dry sand) from conductive anomalies (metal, wet clay, brine) within a controlled soil column. The physics generalizes to full geological profiling; the demo box establishes proof of concept.

Design Lineage Iteration History

The project went through two major design phases before arriving at the final ERT configuration. Each pivot is documented as part of the engineering-process narrative.

Phase 1 — abandoned
FMCW Radar (HLK-LD2410C) + Arduino
Initial design combined FMCW radar for above-ground detection with an ERT subsystem. The radar was rejected because electromagnetic waves at the LD2410C's operating frequency cannot penetrate soil — the dielectric loss is too high. Hardware issues (overheating, possibly from reversed polarity) compounded the decision. The radar subsystem is retained in the documentation as an iteration-narrative artifact showing the design process.
Phase 2 — partially implemented, superseded
ERT with INA128 + IA0505S dual-rail converter
Early ERT design used the INA128 instrumentation amplifier, which requires a dual ±5V supply. A Digi-Key order error (part 296-11837-5-ND resolved to a 74HC563 latch, not an INA128) prevented completion. The IA0505S isolated ±5V converter was ordered to supply the INA128's dual rail. This design was replaced by the single-supply INA333 approach, which is simpler and eliminates the isolated converter entirely.
Phase 3 — current design
ERT with INA333 single-supply + MT3608 compliance rail
The INA333 (GODIYMODULES breakout module) operates single-supply (1.8–5.5V), eliminating the dual-rail converter. Its onboard virtual-ground divider produces a ~1.65V VREF pedestal; the firmware performs a ratiometric subtraction of OUT and VREF to cancel pedestal drift. The MT3608 boost converter supplies ~12V exclusively to the LM324 current pump's V+ rail, providing soil compliance in dry conditions without high voltage entering the 3.3V logic domain.

ERT Theory Physics

Four-electrode measurement principle

ERT exploits the relationship between an injected electrical current and the resulting voltage distribution in the ground. A resistive subsurface material (void, dry sand) produces a larger voltage drop than a conductive one (metal, saturated clay). By sweeping the four-electrode array along the surface and at increasing spacings, a 2D resistivity cross-section is built up.

The four-electrode (Wenner) method separates current injection from voltage measurement. Two current electrodes (A, B) inject a known current I into the ground. Two potential electrodes (M, N) measure the resulting voltage differential. Because M and N draw virtually no current (the INA333 input bias is ~200pA), contact resistance at M and N does not affect the measurement — only bulk resistivity does. Contact resistance at A and B is immaterial because the injected current I is measured directly by the 10Ω sense resistor.

ρ = 2π · a · (VMN / I)
Wenner apparent resistivity formula — a = electrode spacing (m), V = measured M–N voltage (V), I = injected current (A)

Wenner array geometry

In the Wenner configuration, all four electrodes are equally spaced by distance a. The arrangement is: A — M — N — B, each separated by a. Widening a causes the current to penetrate deeper into the ground, sampling greater depths. Each (A, M, N, B) quadruple produces one apparent resistivity reading, plotted at the array midpoint and at a depth proportional to the spacing level.

Pseudo-section

The pseudo-section is the classic ERT visualization: readings at each depth level are plotted as a downward-pointing triangle, with shallow readings at the top and deeper readings below. Each data point is plotted as a colored diamond (or dot) at the horizontal midpoint of the array, one row down per depth level. The color encodes apparent resistivity — blue for conductive, red for resistive — producing a 2D image of the subsurface.

With 8 electrodes at 10cm spacing (80cm array), the instrument produces 2 depth levels, 7 readings total. Upgrading to 16 electrodes (one firmware constant change) produces 5 depth levels and 35 readings — a full pseudo-section triangle.

Geometric factor

The factor 2πa in the Wenner formula is the geometric factor — it converts the ratio V/I (units of Ohms) into apparent resistivity (units of Ω·m). It encodes the geometry of the current flow paths in a uniform half-space. The formula assumes a uniform medium; real soil with anomalies produces "apparent" resistivity that departs from the background, and those departures are the signal.

The 3.3V rule: The Teensy 4.0 is not 5V tolerant. The MT3608 12V rail touches only the LM324 V+ pin — never the Teensy, ADCs, MCP, or INA333. All I²C pull-ups must reference 3.3V, not 5V. Verify with a multimeter before connecting the Teensy.
Common ground is mandatory: The MT3608 output ground, the 3.3V logic ground, and the soil-return electrode must share a single ground node, or the differential measurement will float and return garbage.

Howland Current Pump Injection Stage

The current source is a Howland constant-current pump built from one section of the LM324 quad op-amp, a TL431 voltage reference, and a 2N3904 NPN transistor for current boost. It injects a known, stable current I between electrodes A and B regardless of the soil resistance — up to the compliance limit of the power rail.

Compliance and the MT3608

A constant-current source can only maintain set current if the power rail is high enough to push that current through the total load. Dry soil can present 100kΩ+ of combined contact and bulk resistance. Off a 3.3V rail, the op-amp saturates and current collapses. Off the MT3608 at ~12V, 165µA through 100kΩ requires 16.5V — the pump is on the margin, but lower current (50µA) through the same resistance requires only 5V, which 12V handles with margin.

The formula for required compliance: V_compliance = I_set × (R_contact_A + R_bulk + R_contact_B)

Because the 10Ω sense resistor measures actual injected current, even if the pump cannot hit its set current in very dry soil, the resistivity calculation remains valid — it uses the I that was actually delivered, not the I that was intended.

INA333 Instrumentation Amplifier Measurement

The INA333 (GODIYMODULES breakout module) amplifies the tiny M–N differential voltage — sub-millivolt in typical soil — to a range the ADS1115 can resolve accurately. It was chosen over the INA128 and AD620 after two independent analyses.

The INA333's 200pA bias current is critical: at high electrode impedance (brass in dry soil can be MΩ-range), even nA-level bias produces mV-level offset errors that swamp the signal. The 200pA figure keeps this error below the noise floor.

Gain and the ratiometric approach

The GODIYMODULES module has a fixed onboard R_G = 100Ω, producing:

G = 1 + (100,000 / 100) = 1001
Note: the INA333 uses the 100kΩ numerator (not 50kΩ — that is the INA118, a pin-compatible but different chip)

The module's onboard 10k/10k resistor divider from VCC to GND produces a virtual ground at ~1.65V (for 3.3V supply). This is the VREF pedestal — the output rests at 1.65V when V_MN = 0, and swings ±1.6V around it. The ADS1115 reads both the AMP OUT and the VREF pin. Firmware subtracts them:

V_MN = (V_OUT − V_REF) / G
Ratiometric subtraction — cancels pedestal drift even if VREF moves with temperature or supply ripple

ADC & I²C Bus Digital Chain

ADS1115 — why 16-bit external ADC instead of Teensy onboard

The Teensy 4.0 has a 12-bit ADC (4096 counts over its range). For a 3.3V range, that is ~800µV per count. At G=1001, a 1µV M–N signal produces ~1mV at the amplifier output — 1.25 counts. The noise floor is essentially the signal. The ADS1115 provides 16-bit resolution (65536 counts) with a programmable gain amplifier (PGA) that can narrow the input range to ±0.256V, giving ~7.8µV per count — a 100× improvement in sensitivity over the Teensy ADC.

The ADS1115 also communicates over I²C, keeping the Teensy's pins free and avoiding any 5V-to-3.3V level-shifting on the analog path.

I²C bus rules

  • Teensy 4.0 I²C: pin 18 (SDA0), pin 19 (SCL0)
  • Pull-up resistors: 4.7kΩ from SDA→3.3V and SCL→3.3V. One pair serves all four devices.
  • When using the Uno for bring-up (Stage 1), pull-ups go to 5V — swap to 3.3V before connecting the Teensy.
  • All four devices share a single SDA/SCL bus. I²C is single-master; the Teensy is always master.
  • MCP23017 RESET pins: tie to VDD (3.3V). Do not leave floating.

Electrode Switching — Mux Architecture Switching

Each Wenner reading requires four electrodes to be assigned to roles A, B, M, N. With 8 (or 16) electrodes, any electrode must be routeable to any role. The solution: four CD74HC4067 16-channel analog muxes — one per role — each capable of connecting any of 16 electrode channels to the role's signal path.

Each electrode (0–7 for 8-electrode, 0–15 for 16) connects to channel C[electrode_index] on all four muxes simultaneously. Select pins S0–S3 are driven by the MCP23017 with the binary-encoded channel number. The enable pin (EN) is active-LOW: driving EN HIGH disconnects all channels on that mux (high-Z), which is how unused roles are cleared.

BSS138 level shifters: The CD74HC4067 operates at 3.3V logic (Teensy domain), fed from the MCP23017 which also runs at 3.3V. No level shifting is needed in the final Teensy build — the BSS138 modules in the BOM are available insurance if any 5V signal path is encountered during bring-up.

Measurement sequence (per reading)

1

Clear all roles

Drive all four mux EN pins HIGH (disable). Every electrode is now high-Z — no current flows.

2

Assign roles

Drive Mux A to the A electrode channel, Mux B to B, Mux M to M, Mux N to N. Enable each mux (EN → LOW).

3

Settle

Wait SETTLE_MS (50ms) for transients to die. Current begins flowing through soil.

4

Read ADS1115 0x48

Read A0 (INA333 OUT) and A1 (VREF), each averaged over SAMPLES_PER_READING. Compute: V_MN = (A0 − A1) / INA_GAIN.

5

Read ADS1115 0x49

Read differential A0/A1 (across 10Ω sense resistor), averaged. Compute: I = V_sense / 10.0. Fallback to ASSUMED_CURRENT_A if 0x49 absent.

6

Compute resistivity

ρ = 2π × spacing × (V_MN / I). Store as a Reading struct.

CSV output format

The full scan outputs a 9-column CSV between BEGIN_SCAN / END_SCAN markers:

a_idx, m_idx, n_idx, b_idx, level, spacing_m, vmn_V, current_A, resistivity_ohm_m

This format is directly compatible with the HTML pseudo-section viewer (paste CSV, click "Render section").

Synthesis Steps — Breadboard Build Build Guide

Build in this order. Each stage is verified before proceeding. A fault found at stage N is isolated to stage N; a fault found at the end is found in a tangle of everything.

0

Power rails — no ICs plugged in

Time: ~30 min. Wire the MT3608 alone: power bank → MT3608 IN+ (5V) and IN− (GND). Multimeter on MT3608 OUT. Turn the trimpot clockwise slowly until the meter reads 12.0V. Stop. Label this wire. Do not connect it to anything else yet. Confirm the Teensy 3V3 pin produces 3.3V (power the Teensy by USB only, no shields). Establish two clearly separated rails: 12V (orange wire) and 3.3V (red wire).

Verify before proceeding: 12V on boost output, 3.3V on logic rail, 0V between the two ground buses (they're common), no bus bars touching each other.

1

I²C bus bring-up on the Arduino Uno (5V)

Time: ~45 min. Place: Uno, both ADS1115 (0x48/0x49), both MCP23017 (0x20/0x21). Wire: Uno 5V → breadboard rails. Uno GND → breadboard GND. Uno A4 → SDA bus, Uno A5 → SCL bus. Two 4.7kΩ resistors: SDA→5V and SCL→5V. ADS1115 #1 ADDR→GND (0x48). ADS1115 #2 ADDR→VDD (0x49). MCP23017 #1: RESET→5V, A0/A1/A2→GND (0x20). MCP23017 #2: RESET→5V, A0→VDD, A1/A2→GND (0x21). Upload the I²C scanner. Expected output: 0x20, 0x21, 0x48, 0x49 all found. If any missing: re-seat module, check VDD and ADDR pin.

2

Mux wiring and self-test

Time: ~90 min total (start tonight, finish Saturday morning). Add the four CD74HC4067 muxes. Wire each mux: VCC→3.3V (or 5V for Uno phase), GND→GND. Wire MCP23017 GPA0–GPA3 to Mux A S0–S3. GPA4 → Mux A EN. MCP GPB0–GPB3 → Mux M S0–S3. GPB4 → Mux M EN. Repeat for MCP 0x21 → Mux B and Mux N. Wire 4 short jumpers as test electrodes: electrode 0–3 on C0–C3 of all four muxes. Upload firmware. Run 't' (self-test). Probe Mux A SIG pin with meter while test cycles — should see continuity appear/disappear. Repeat for B, M, N.

3

Current pump — Howland + TL431 + 2N3904

Time: ~90 min. Highest-risk stage. Build on breadboard: LM324 V+ → 12V rail (the MT3608 output). LM324 V− → GND. Build TL431 divider (10k/10k from 3.3V to GND) → ~1.65V ref → LM324 IN+. Build Howland network (R1=R2=R3=R4=10kΩ 0.1%). Wire 2N3904: base → LM324 OUT via 1kΩ; collector → 12V via 100Ω; emitter → test load. Connect 10kΩ test resistor between emitter and GND. Wire 10Ω sense resistor in series between the load and GND. Measure: multimeter across the 10Ω — should read ~1.65mV (165µA injection). If 0V: LM324 not getting 12V (check pin 4). If railing to 12V: Howland network mismatch, verify resistors individually.

4

INA333 — solder to perfboard, ratiometric read

Time: ~90 min. Solder this stage — do NOT breadboard INA333. Solder the INA333 module to a perfboard section. VCC → 3.3V, GND → GND. IN+ and IN− → the M and N test nodes (initially a resistor divider off the pump output). OUT → ADS1115 0x48 A0. VREF (module's mid-supply output) → ADS1115 0x48 A1. Place 0.1µF ceramic cap between VCC and GND, close to the module. Upload firmware, run 'l' (live mode). You should see Vmn_mV reading a non-zero stable value, I_uA reading from the sense resistor, rho_ohm_m finite. If Vmn_mV = 0: INA333 power or wiring. If random huge: VREF pin not wired. If rho = NaN: current reading zero, check 0x49 wiring.

5

Migrate to Teensy 4.0

Time: ~30 min. Remove the Uno. Place the Teensy. Teensy 3V3 → 3.3V rail. Teensy GND → GND. Teensy pin 18 → SDA, pin 19 → SCL. Critical: remove the 5V pull-up resistors and replace with pull-ups to 3.3V. Re-upload the firmware (Arduino IDE + Teensyduino, Board = Teensy 4.0). Run 't' self-test — all four I²C addresses should still answer. Run 'l' live mode — same numbers as with the Uno = clean migration. If a device drops off: one of the pull-ups is still on 5V, or a device needs a supply pin reconnected to 3.3V.

6

Soil box — first real scan

Time: ~60 min. Insert all 8 brass rods into the demo box using the electrode-spacing jig (10cm apart). Connect rod cables to mux C0–C7. Run 'l' live — watch ρ change as you move the buried target. Then run 'g' for the full Wenner scan. Copy CSV from serial monitor, paste into the HTML viewer, click "Render section." You should see a pseudo-section with at least one visible color anomaly. If flat (everything same color): wet the soil and re-run. If NaN: current not flowing through soil — check electrode contact and 12V rail.

7

Collect poster data — three soil states

Time: ~2–3 hours. Run three full scans with identical buried targets: (1) dry sand background, (2) damp soil (spray bottle), (3) wet/saturated near the injection electrodes. Screenshot the pseudo-section viewer for each. Record for each: set current (from Howland design), measured I from 0x49, raw V_MN from 'l' mode, computed ρ background. Problems Addressed Engineering Challenges

Problem 1: Dual-rail amplifier supply

The original INA128 required a dual ±5V supply. Generating ±5V isolated from a single USB power bank in a compact enclosure required the IA0505S SIP converter — an additional failure point, a cost item, and a PCB complexity increase. Solution: The INA333 operates single-supply from 1.8V to 5.5V, using an internal virtual-ground architecture. Deletes the IA0505S entirely.

Problem 2: Current pump compliance in dry soil

Dry sand can present >100kΩ of total injection-path resistance. At 3.3V supply, the LM324 saturates before reaching the set current; the measured I collapses to near-zero, making valid resistivity computation impossible. Moisture-dependent demos are fragile. Solution: The MT3608 boost converter provides ~12V to the LM324 V+ pin only (the rest of the system stays at 3.3V). This provides sufficient compliance to inject into dry soil. Because I is always measured (not assumed), accuracy is unconditional even at partial compliance.

Problem 3: 5V tolerance and Teensy pin safety

The Teensy 4.0 is not 5V tolerant on any pin. Many common I²C modules default to 5V pull-ups, and the Arduino Uno used in bring-up runs at 5V. A single 5V signal reaching a Teensy pin can cause permanent damage. Solution: I²C bring-up is done on the 5V Uno first (stages 1–4); migration to the Teensy at stage 5 includes a mandatory step to swap all pull-up resistors from 5V to 3.3V before the Teensy is connected. The MT3608 12V rail is labeled and physically separated.

Problem 4: ADC resolution

The Teensy 4.0's onboard 12-bit ADC resolves ~800µV per count at 3.3V range. At G=1001, a 1µV M–N signal produces ~1mV — just over 1 count. The SNR is insufficient for geophysical measurement. Solution: ADS1115 16-bit ADC with programmable gain. At PGA ±0.256V, resolution is ~7.8µV per count — ~100× better. Two units: one for the measurement chain, one for the sense resistor.

Problem 5: Electrode contact resistance

Brass rods in dry soil develop high contact resistance (can reach MΩ). This has no effect on M and N (sense electrodes draw ~200pA — contact resistance × 200pA ≈ 0V error). It does reduce compliance for A and B (injection electrodes), addressed by the MT3608 boost rail. Additional mitigation: Crimp terminations (not solder) to rods; ring terminals for reliable electrical contact. Deeper insertion and firmer soil packing around injection electrodes for demo runs.

Problem 6: INA333 pedestal drift

The virtual-ground VREF pedestal at ~1.65V can drift with temperature, supply ripple, or component aging. If the firmware treats VREF as a fixed constant (e.g., 1.65V), any drift in the actual VREF translates directly to a reading offset error. Solution: Ratiometric measurement. The firmware reads the live VREF voltage from ADS1115 A1 and subtracts it from the live AMP OUT at A0. Drift cancels regardless of cause or magnitude.

Problem 7: INA333 at G=1001 on breadboard

Standard solderless breadboards have parasitic resistance of 10–100MΩ between adjacent rows. At G=1001, even pA-level leakage currents through this resistance produce offset voltages that swamp a sub-mV signal. Solution: The INA333 stage is soldered to perfboard. This is the only stage that cannot be left on the breadboard for the prototype build.

Key Engineering Decisions Rationale

Wenner over dipole-dipole or Schlumberger

Three common ERT electrode configurations exist. The Wenner array was chosen for the following reasons: equal electrode spacing makes the geometric factor calculation unambiguous (2πa, not a more complex expression); all four electrodes move together as a unit (simpler mux truth table); the Wenner array has the best signal-to-noise ratio of the three for a given current injection; and it is the textbook ERT configuration that judges will recognize.

I²C over SPI for ADC and GPIO expander

I²C requires only two wires (SDA + SCL) shared by all devices, versus SPI's requirement for a separate chip-select per device. With four addressable devices (two ADS1115, two MCP23017), I²C reduces the Teensy pin requirement dramatically. The ADS1115's 860 SPS maximum sample rate over I²C is more than sufficient for averaging 16 samples per reading.

MCP23017 over direct Teensy GPIO for mux control

Each of four CD74HC4067 muxes requires 5 control pins (S0–S3 + EN) = 20 GPIO total. The Teensy 4.0 has insufficient pins for this without sacrificing I²C, UART, and USB. Two MCP23017 expanders add 32 GPIO over two I²C addresses, leaving the Teensy's native pins for communication only.

Measured I over assumed I

Commercial ERT systems measure actual injected current rather than relying on the theoretical set point. This is the correct engineering choice: the Howland pump is not perfect, contact resistance varies, and compliance limits apply. The 10Ω 1% sense resistor on the second ADS1115 makes every resistivity reading valid regardless of whether the pump hit its set point. The formula ρ = 2πa·(V/I) is only accurate if I is measured.

Two-board stack with analog/digital split

The MT3608 switching regulator and current injection path generate switching noise that can couple into the sub-mV M–N signal path if they share copper. The two-board stack physically separates the noisy analog/power domain (Board 2: muxes, INA333, pump, ADCs, MT3608) from the clean digital domain (Board 1: Teensy, MCPs, I²C bus). A single star-ground tie point prevents ground loops. This is a real EMC engineering argument, not aesthetic organization.

Impact & Applications Significance

Primary application: post-disaster housing safety

Following earthquakes, floods, and landslides, displaced communities often rebuild on whatever ground is available, without knowledge of subsurface soil liquefaction zones, voids, or unstable fill layers. Commercial ERT surveys are inaccessible in price and logistics. This instrument can be operated by non-specialists (electrode insertion + a laptop), making ground-truth data available to communities that currently have none. Target regions: rural South and Southeast Asia, where post-disaster informal housing reconstruction is common.

Secondary applications

  • Archaeology and paleontology: Non-destructive detection of buried artifacts, foundations, and fossil-bearing strata. The instrument's $245 cost enables systematic site surveys that are currently impractical.
  • Agricultural monitoring: Soil moisture mapping and root-zone characterization without soil sampling.
  • Infrastructure inspection: Detection of voids under roads, erosion beneath foundations, and sinkhole precursors.
  • Educational platform: The open firmware and documented design make it a complete, buildable ERT educational instrument. Cost is within reach of university lab budgets in developing countries.

Lineage to professional systems

The measurement physics in this instrument is identical to the ABEM Terrameter and AGI SuperSting product lines — the same four-electrode Wenner method, the same Wenner geometric factor (2πa), the same CSV data structure. This is not a toy or demonstration model: it is a cost-reduced implementation of a mature technique. The primary engineering contribution is identifying the subset of commodity parts that achieves sufficient measurement quality (16-bit ADC, 200pA in-amp bias, G=1001, 12V compliance) for the target application while staying within a $250 build budget.

Connection to NASA RIMFAX and ESA WISDOM

NASA's RIMFAX instrument on the Perseverance Mars rover and ESA's WISDOM on ExoMars both perform subsurface imaging using electromagnetic methods. This project's design process — pivoting from RF/radar (electromagnetic) to DC resistivity (ERT) when soil penetration proved infeasible — mirrors a real design decision class in geophysical instrumentation. The iteration narrative is directly analogous to instrument development trade studies.

One-sentence summary for any documentation

"A sub-$250 electrical resistivity tomography instrument that maps subsurface density anomalies using a Wenner four-electrode array, a 16-bit ADC measurement chain, and a single-supply instrumentation amplifier — making a previously $5,000–$50,000 technique accessible to communities performing post-disaster ground safety assessment."
